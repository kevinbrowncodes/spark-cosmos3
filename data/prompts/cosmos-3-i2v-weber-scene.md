## Cosmos is a physics model â give it real, full-body motion

Cosmos 3 is a world-action model: it renders physical dynamics from a frame, so it can handle real, full-body motion â standing up, walking, crossing a room, reaching, working with objects, wading, interacting with the setting. Give it genuine action to render; don't shrink the motion to play safe. The only limits are real ones: the motion must be something the real world would actually do (bodies don't teleport, contort, detach limbs, or pass through solid objects), and bigger or faster motion renders less reliably than slower motion â so give ambitious action enough of the 10 seconds to happen at a believable pace. With that in mind:

- **Give every clip real, purposeful motion.** Every clip should show the subject actually doing something that moves â reaching, standing, stepping, working with an object, walking, wading, toweling off â not idling in place. - **One continuous through-line per clip, at a believable pace.** A clip can carry a fuller action now â rise, turn, and step to the counter as one connected motion â as long as it flows in real physical order rather than a jump-cut of unrelated events crammed into one beat. - **Keep motion to what a real body actually does** â a stand, a step, a walk, a stretch, a reach, a turn, a crouch are all fine; only genuine impossibilities are off-limits (a joint rotating past its range, a limb detaching, the body passing through a solid counter or wall).

## What you MUST put in every motion instruction

The upsampler does NOT automatically enforce most video constraints â it only enforces image-anchoring, a timestamped timeline, audio direction, media controls, timing, first-frame match, and preserving facts you state. Everything else is on you. So every motion instruction must explicitly include (as global lines after the three beats, unless noted):

- **Camera behavior â state it every clip (follow or hold, NEVER zoom).** Say what the camera does as one global line, because if you stay silent the upsampler invents its own move. When the subject moves through the space â stands, steps, crosses to the counter, walks, wades â the camera should FOLLOW or reframe to keep them well-composed: a gentle handheld follow, a lateral track alongside, a pan or a tilt, the way Weber's own films moved with their subjects. When the moment is still, a held camera is right. **NEVER zoom, push in, or pull out â no change of framing scale, ever.** The camera keeps the subject at a roughly constant size; it moves WITH the subject rather than zooming toward or away from them. Two reasons this is a hard rule: pulling out (going wider) forces the model to invent the newly-revealed edges of the frame â a prime source of missing or garbled detail â and any punch-in you actually want is cleaner and fully under your control when the editor adds it in post, so there's no reason to bake it into the generation. Beyond that, the move must be motivated by the subject's action; a camera that drifts on its own while the subject stands still is the classic AI tell. To keep the scene one-directional, prefer moving the camera WITH or AHEAD of the subject â a clean, safe pattern is the camera retreating ahead of a forward-walking subject, so it only ever shows new space in front of him (and his face) while the space he leaves falls away behind and is never re-shown. Don't pan, swing, or track the camera back to an area it already covered; once a spot is behind the action, leave it there. Keep the camera direction in the global line, never inside a timed beat.
- **A clean, stable ending frame with the face to the lens â required (person subjects).** The final timed beat (`[0:08-0:10]`) must bring the motion to a settled, in-focus pause, and for any person in frame it must land with the **face forward and clear â turned toward the camera, unobstructed, with the mouth closed/neutral** â nothing mid-blur, mid-transition, or mid-fast-motion. This is the single most important rule for identity: a clear, frontal face is the most stable anchor the model has, and ending on it **prevents face drift** â the gradual morphing of the person's features that otherwise creeps in. In SCENE MODE it matters doubly, because this final frame becomes the seed for the next clip, so any drift here compounds down the chain. This is the one hard rule you always keep: wherever the action has taken the subject, the final beat brings them to rest with the **face square and frontal to the camera â head not turned, both eyes open and clear**, but with a **soft, indirect gaze just past the lens** (as if unaware of the camera â NOT a locked stare down the barrel, which reads as watched and posed). The safeguard that protects identity is the FRONTAL FACE, so the head must stay square â do not turn it away; only the eyes go soft and slightly off-axis (keep the gaze just past the lens, not into the far distance, so the eyes stay near-centered). Through 0:00â0:08 the eyes stay on the action, the task, or the other person, off the lens entirely; on the settle the face comes frontal with that soft, indirect look. The frontal-face landing is the anchor against face drift; the soft gaze is what keeps it candid instead of a lens-stare. Only skip the forward-face landing if the seed's subject is genuinely turned away or faceless (a back, a silhouette, a non-person subject) â then settle to the cleanest, stillest equivalent. In single-clip mode the clip still ends here, so bring the face square and frontal with that soft, indirect gaze rather than leaving it turned away.
- **Hands travel on skin, props, or a companion; the waistband is a tease line, not a reveal (person subjects).** Hands may move naturally, travel over bare skin (chest, abs, arms, shoulders, neck, jaw, hair), handle a towel or prop, rest on an animal, or graze/hook a low waistband as a suggestive beat â but must NOT pull clothing lower, off, or aside to expose, and must NOT settle on the crotch as a focus. Suggest more; never show it. Name where each hand ends up and that it settles, so nothing free-floats (finger clipping). Use the subject's own left/right. (Motion right at the waistband can still trigger the render's clothing/guardrail behavior â keep it a graze, not a pull.)
- **Mouth closed and still (when a person is present) â state it explicitly.** POSITIVELY state that the mouth stays closed and still, lips relaxed, throughout the clip. Silence about the mouth lets the model add idle lip motion or lip-sync on its own. Put an explicit "his/her mouth stays closed and still, no talking or lip movement" global line into every script with a person (unless the run explicitly requests dialogue). A closed-mouth, heavy-lidded expression is fine and on-aesthetic. (An easy, relaxed half-smile is also on-aesthetic for Weber â but only as a still expression, not a laugh or a moving mouth.) If the activity naturally uses the mouth (a sip of coffee, a bite), keep it to a single slow, brief motion â raise the mug and pause at the lips, or one unhurried sip â never sustained chewing or gulping, because the generator turns lingering mouth motion into lip-sync or talking; when in doubt, pick an activity beat that needs no open mouth (cradling the coffee and breathing the steam reads just as well).
- **Setting-specific ambient sound only, no voices â state it explicitly.** The generator produces audio, and any vocal-like sound makes a person's lips move to match â and worse, vague or reverberant audio descriptions ("echoes," "reverberant room") make the model fill in faint *distant voices*. - **Let the subject move through the space â the frame is not a cage.** The subject is free to stand, turn, step, cross the room, change distance from the camera, and go wherever the activity leads (the counter, the doorway, the water) â the camera follows or reframes to keep them composed (see the camera line). Don't shrink an action to keep the original framing; move the subject and let the camera go with them.

## Common failure modes and the fix for each

Each item names the positive thing to write; the explanation covers the failure it prevents.

- **Fill the clip with a real action â don't idle, and don't fall back on flexing.** The failure is a clip that just breathes and shifts in place, wasting the 10 seconds. Give the subject a genuine action that moves through space â reach, stand, step, work the coffee, cross to the counter, towel off, greet the dog. If no action comes to mind, that's a cue to look harder at the props and setting, not a cue to add flexes and torso-touching to feel active (that stacked self-touch move is the Mizer drift).
- **Live the moment, don't pose it (the #1 Weber failure).** If every clip is "a flex or a hand over the torso + a smoldering stare to camera," it has become a Mizer physique reel â generic studio thirst, the opposite of Weber. Lead instead with real, lived action or restful stillness: a reach for something in the frame, a turn caught mid-movement, a lean, a hand through the hair, an interaction with the setting or a companion â so let the sensuality ride those actions â pushed to the R-ceiling â rather than freezing into a stop-and-pose tease at the lens. Don't end every clip on a to-camera smolder; let the face read candid, and often let the eyes rest off-lens mid-moment. Even a quiet seed usually affords a small real action â reach for the coffee, open the window, greet the dog, pull on a shirt â so reach for that before you settle for stillness. - **Don't hallucinate water, sweat, or wind.** Feature water/sweat/wind ONLY when the seed already shows it (wet skin, visible sweat, surf, a pool, a shower, hair already moving) or the frame plainly contains the means to make it (standing in the shallows, a running tap, an open windswept shore). - **Objects don't pop in â only handle what's already in the frame.** The freedom to move and use props has one hard companion rule: the subject can only pick up, carry, or use objects genuinely visible in the seed (or established in a prior clip). Reaching for an off-frame or not-in-the-image object makes the model conjure it â the classic pop-in. - **Keep the mouth closed and silent by default (person subjects).** Unless dialogue is explicitly requested, the person stays silent and the lips stay still. - **Move clothing and loose material only through real forces.** Clothing, hair, water, and cloth shift only as a direct result of motion or forces present in the scene (the subject's own movement, wind already in the frame, gravity acting on something falling). - **Tease the line, don't cross it (person subjects).** A stretch, a hand tracing the torso, or a thumb grazing a low waistband reads charged and R-rated; pulling clothing down, exposing, or settling on the crotch reads explicit and trips guardrails. - **Name only the motion â leave appearance to the upsampler.** Whatever the scene contains, the upsampler already reads its appearance from the seed image. Write only what MOVES and when; the charge comes from the *motion* (the wade, the stretch, the wet hair, the tease), not from ogling description.
- **Keep eyes open through the motion (person subjects).** Anchor on an open-eyed, heavy-lidded state rather than a closed-eye or mid-blink moment, unless a slow blink is itself the natural predicted motion.
- **Keep readable text and tiny objects incidental.** The model morphs small lettering and fine detail over time, so keep any visible text (a logo, a waistband brand) in the background of the motion rather than its focus.
- **Don't over-articulate hair or fine detail.** The model renders hair and fine texture unreliably under motion, so let the main gesture carry the clip rather than centering it on hair or tiny detail. - **Don't invent a narrative twist or hard action.** Weber's frames don't resolve through plot â they capture a warm, real moment. Favor slow candid motion over dramatic beats or sudden events.
